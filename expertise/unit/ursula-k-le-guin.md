## How they work
- Invented worlds built from real study, then let go: for the ice trek in *The Left Hand of Darkness* she read Scott, Shackleton and Cherry-Garrard, "and I had to plan out the trek across the Gobrin Ice just as carefully as Estraven did, ounce by ounce and day by day." (Library of America Q&A, 2017)
- A body of work as a landscape, not a line: the Hainish books are "more like a forest than a tree" (Library of America Q&A, 2017).
- Method stated plainly: "Science fiction imagines other places, other times, other beings and ways of being, by using the tools of realism and working almost entirely within its limits." (Library of America Q&A, 2017)
- Material: essays and speeches she released for free reuse; Taoism (her own Lao Tzu rendering) and anthropology (her parents were Alfred and Theodora Kroeber) behind the fiction.

## Hooks to the brief
- **Sacred / profane:** the carrier bag puts the container, not the weapon, first; the book page lists "the home, the shrine, the place that contains whatever is sacred" among its holders. A ritual object can be read as a bag before it is read as a spear.
- **Ritual:** *Always Coming Home* is a worked example of the Book of Rituals form: a people documented through their ceremonies, drawings and songs rather than a plot.
- **Time:** the Mills address asks the graduates to live in "the dark that nourishes" rather than the light; the night side is where "the future is". A timeline can run down as well as forward.
- **Non-human:** "the earth is our country"; blessing is looked for "around, and down", not up.
- **Power:** "without the need to dominate, and without the need to be dominated"; the NBF speech names the market as a power that, like kings, can be resisted. Dunne borrows her "realists of a larger reality" for design (see the Dunne & Raby card).

## How they work
No outline: "I work intuitively and will follow a trail of documents or my instincts until the project emerges." Deep encounters with archival material (inventories, social surveys, police and reform-school records, photographs), then writing "with and against the archive". *Wayward Lives* began with one photograph of a girl taken in 1882 and took about seven years; it carries 61 images, many of them classic slum photographs given new captions, and uses Du Bois's *The Philadelphia Negro* "to other ends" (2019 talk, auto-captions, 00:36:11–00:38:00). The text is built to be polyphonic, a chorus, with the sound of the street and the hallway in it. Teaching is part of the method: the classroom as "a laboratory in thought". Dancers and theatre-makers (Selina Thompson's *salt.*) have built work from her texts.

## Hooks to the brief
- **Sacred / profane:** Pleasure as refusal: "The forms of pleasure and joy and beauty that are also the focus here are those moments that are refusals and ruptures with the order" (2020 White Review); joy as "self-dissolution, escape".
- **Ritual:** The circle as "a space of sociality and radical thought", traced from Frederick Douglass to the Bakongo cosmogram (2020 White Review): a ritual form that travels with a diaspora.
- **Time:** "temporal entanglement" (2018): a timeline where 1919 and 2019 sit on the same line, "it doesn’t matter if it’s 1919 or 2019" (2020 White Review).
- **Place:** An archive that is "one of landscape", "an archive of the unsaid" (2020 White Review): the slave route in Ghana read as site; the tenement hallway, the alley and the block as the architecture of *Wayward Lives*.
- **Power:** Du Bois's survey as the start of the "black slum" as a category (2018); the plantation as "an enclosure", and "It requires abolition, the abolition of the carceral world, the abolition of capitalism." (2020 Artforum).

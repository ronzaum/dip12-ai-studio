## How they work
Blog posts written within days of an event: the 2015 election post went up four days after the vote and opens with a friend's election-night message. Evidence is popular culture read as symptom: music, TV, pubs, royal crowds. Lectures restate a book's argument through music history (what 1975 sounded like against 2005). In Good for Nothing he puts his own depression on the page as evidence for a political claim, then reads a therapist (David Smail) to test it. Posts end in instructions: talk to fellow workers about how you feel; do not blame yourself. Worked with Justin Barton on the audio-essay On Vanishing Land (noted on the k-punk index page; not saved).

## Hooks to the brief
- **Ritual:** English rituals of consumption and deference as symptoms: "people will camp out for three days to glimpse the Royal Couple" (Abandon hope, 2015).
- **Time:** years that fade into one another like non-places (00:10:40); a "politics of of time" (00:39:13; lecture captions are automatic) against the attack on "that time free of urgencies" (00:39:27). A ritual drawn in time can show which time it keeps.
- **Place:** the non-place (airports, retail parks) is his model for the non-time; in London an open sense of time is "now kind of radically fugitive" (00:40:09).
- **Power:** responsibilisation: each member of the subordinate class "is encouraged into feeling that their poverty, lack of opportunities, or unemployment, is their fault and their fault alone" (Good for Nothing, 2014). A conflict section can show who is made to carry the blame.

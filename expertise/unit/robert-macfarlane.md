## How they work
Walks, descends, sails: one journey per notebook, more than twenty for *Underland*, which took eight years. Paper, not phone: "phones smash, while notebooks bend." The notebooks hold biro sketches, transcriptions of conversations, and things tucked in (feathers, mica); mud stains and smudges count as part of the record. Collects place words from crofters, farmers, sailors, maps, glossaries and letters, and sorts them into glossaries by terrain type. For the river book he treats the rivers as co-authors and greets them when he meets them. Works with musicians and artists (Delia Stevens and Will Pound on an orchestral piece; the sculptor Steve Dilworth gave him the whalebone owl that went on every *Underland* journey).

## Hooks to the brief
- **Sacred / profane:** The nuclear-waste warning text he calls "pitched somewhere between confession, caution and black mass": a design brief for a sign that must hold for 24,000 years (2016 essay).
- **Ritual:** He greets rivers when he meets them, and "answer comes there none" (2025 interview): a ritual with a non-human addressee.
- **Time:** The Anthropocene "requires that we imagine ourselves inhabitants not just of a human lifetime or generation, but also of “deep time”" (2016 essay); a timeline can run from the Permian to 2038.
- **Non-human:** Rivers as "who", not "which"; "to river" as a verb; rights-of-nature law "to train human beings to listen, pay attention to, and learn from, rivers" (2025 interview).
- **Place:** "A place literacy is leaving us" (2015 essay): a site's lost words are site evidence; the Peat Glossary of Lewis is a model for a Context section.
- **Power:** "forest" as land set aside for hunting, "tangled up in political histories of access and landownership" (2015 essay); and the Anthropocene critique that "the poor and the dispossessed are far more in it than others" (2016 essay).

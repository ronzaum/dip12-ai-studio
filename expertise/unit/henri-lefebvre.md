## How they work
Starts from concepts and goes to the concrete: "we follow the second, more philosophical method, with its attendant risks" (p. 5). Listens from a window over a Paris junction: a balcony or window gives the inside-and-outside position the method needs (p. 27). Counts what repeats: traffic lights, school runs, shoppers, the night that slows rhythms but does not stop them. Works with pairs of opposites, above all the cyclical against the linear, and repetition against difference (pp. 6–8). Collaborator: his wife Catherine Régulier, co-author of the 1985 rhythmanalysis essays (Michon, 2019). Time: wrote on rhythm from 1961 to the posthumous book of 1992 (Michon, 2019).

## Hooks to the brief
- **Sacred / profane:** Public rhythms are "Calendars, fêtes, ceremonies and celebrations" (p. 18); rural society "was also the society of the fête, of festivities. But that aspect, the best, has been lost" ("The Right to the City"). Ask what fête your conflict has lost, and who profits from its absence.
- **Ritual:** Rites repeat, but each repetition lets in "something new and unforeseen" (p. 6). Film the ritual for its differences, not its sameness.
- **Time:** Cyclical rhythms come from "days, nights, seasons" and linear ones from "the monotony of actions and of movements, imposed structures" (p. 8). A time-based drawing can lay the two over each other at your site and mark where they clash.
- **Place:** Rhythm needs "a place, a time and an expenditure of energy" (p. 15). Pick the window or balcony from which the ritual can be seen and heard whole, and draw from there.
- **Power:** The city needs "places of simultaneity and encounters, places where exchange would not go through exchange value, commerce and profit" ("The Right to the City"). Mark on the site drawing which places still allow that.
